# Role 3 - Data Fit

## Shared starting point

- **Selected project area:** Community Safety Resource Planning using current LAPD NIBRS offense data and legacy LA crime data
- **Candidate Vision A:** A Community Safety Partnership Bureau manager at the Los Angeles Police Department must decide which geographic areas and time periods should be prioritized for a pilot expansion of non-enforcement community outreach resources.
- **Candidate Vision B:** A street-lighting program manager at the City of Los Angeles Bureau of Street Lighting must decide which geographic areas with recurring nighttime reported offenses should be prioritized for on-site lighting assessments.
- **Core data sources and original links:** [Current LAPD NIBRS Offenses Dataset](https://data.lacity.org/Public-Safety/LAPD-NIBRS-Offenses-Dataset/k7nn-b2ep) and [Legacy LA Crime Data, 2020–2024](https://data.lacity.org/Public-Safety/Crime-Data-from-2020-to-Present/2nrs-mtv8)

## What the core data actually contains

- **One row represents:** The two core files have different row meanings. In the current NIBRS file, one row is one NIBRS offense record identified by `UniqueNIBRNo`; one `CaseNo` can contribute multiple offense rows. In the legacy file, one row is one legacy crime record identified by `DR_NO`; the primary offense is in `Crm Cd`, while additional offense codes can appear in `Crm Cd 2`, `Crm Cd 3`, and `Crm Cd 4`. A row in one file is therefore not directly equivalent to a row in the other.
- **People, places, events, and periods observed:** Both files describe offenses reported to LAPD in the City of Los Angeles and include occurrence/report dates, occurrence time, LAPD area, reporting district, privacy-protected location, coordinates, offense categories, premise, status, and selected victim or incident attributes. The legacy file provides historical records with occurrence dates from 2020 through 2024. The current NIBRS file primarily provides records from the new RMS period in 2024–2026, while also containing some earlier occurrence dates for records later entered into RMS.
- **Important identifiers:** NIBRS uses `UniqueNIBRNo` for an offense row and `CaseNo` for a case. Legacy uses `DR_NO` for a row and stores possible additional offenses in separate crime-code columns. Both contain area and reporting-district identifiers, but capitalization differs: NIBRS uses `RPT Dist No`, while Legacy uses `Rpt Dist No`.
- **Important measures:** NIBRS includes `TotalOffenseCount` and `TotalVictimCount`; Legacy includes victim age and up to four crime-code fields but does not use the same NIBRS offense-row structure. In both files, occurrence date/time and geographic fields support descriptive pattern analysis. Raw row counts across the two systems are not a comparable measure unless the team first defines a common unit and validates the mapping.
- **Time coverage:** The downloaded Legacy file contains 1,004,894 rows with occurrence dates from January 1, 2020 through December 30, 2024. The downloaded NIBRS file contains 471,285 rows and is concentrated in 2024–2026. The files overlap in 2024: Legacy has 87,438 rows on or after the March 7, 2024 RMS start date, while NIBRS has 102,969 offense rows with 2024 occurrence dates. The overlap and reporting-system change prevent a simple append into one continuous trend.
- **Known missingness or quality limits:** All 1,004,894 Legacy `DR_NO` values and all 471,285 NIBRS `UniqueNIBRNo` values are nonmissing and unique. Legacy has 588 missing `Premis Desc` values, 16 missing `Premis Cd` values, and 2,240 rows with zero latitude or longitude. NIBRS has 7,416 missing `Date Rptd`, 557 missing `NIBR Code`, 48 missing `Premise Desc`, 132 missing latitude/longitude pairs, 547 zero-coordinate rows, 14,748 `Time OCC` values equal to `0000`, and 135 occurrence dates before 2000. Locations are privacy-rounded rather than exact incident points. The two files also use different crime classifications and reporting structures.

## Candidate Vision A

- **Exact columns that could provide evidence:** Both files provide occurrence dates/times, `AREA`, `AREA NAME`, reporting district, `LOCATION`, `LAT`, `LON`, offense descriptions/codes, and premise descriptions. NIBRS can be counted by `UniqueNIBRNo` or distinct `CaseNo`. Legacy can be counted by unique `DR_NO`, with `Crm Cd 1–4` reviewed so that additional offenses are not ignored. Together these fields can describe where and when reported-offense patterns appear in the historical and current systems.
- **Decision-important information not observed:** Neither file contains existing outreach-program locations, service capacity, staffing, costs, resident requests, community priorities, participation, neighborhood population denominators, or evidence that a particular outreach program improves safety. They also do not measure unreported offenses or fully explain differences in public reporting and police recording.
- **What the data supports:** The files support separate, descriptive summaries of reported-offense patterns by LAPD area, reporting district, privacy-protected location, date, time, offense category, and premise type. Legacy can provide historical context for 2020–2024, while NIBRS can support current analysis using its more detailed offense structure. The results should be shown as separate reporting eras unless a valid crosswalk and comparable counting unit are established.
- **What is only partly supported:** The data can identify locations and periods for further community review, but recorded-offense frequency is only one input to outreach prioritization. It does not establish unmet community need, service gaps, community acceptance, or likely program effectiveness. A trend across 2024 may also reflect the reporting-system transition rather than a real-world change.
- **What is outside the data's scope:** The team cannot use these files alone to make a final outreach allocation, estimate staffing or budget, rank residents' needs, or predict pilot effects. Those decisions require service, population, community-input, cost, capacity, and outcome data.
- **Association or causation:** The analysis can describe associations between reported-offense patterns and observed place, time, offense, or premise fields. It cannot claim that limited outreach caused offenses or that expanded outreach would reduce them because neither file observes outreach exposure, intervention assignment, comparison groups, or program outcomes.

## Candidate Vision B

- **Exact columns that could provide evidence:** Both files include occurrence date/time, LAPD area, reporting district, `LOCATION`, `LAT`, `LON`, offense description/code, and premise description. These fields can support separate nighttime-location screens for Legacy and NIBRS. Legacy `DR_NO` and NIBRS `CaseNo`/`UniqueNIBRNo` must retain their separate meanings when records are counted.
- **Decision-important information not observed:** Neither file contains streetlight or pole locations, illumination measurements, outage and maintenance records, lighting ownership, nighttime pedestrian or traffic volume, visibility, vegetation, site condition, installation cost, or inspection history. They do not show the lighting conditions present when an offense occurred.
- **What the data supports:** The team can apply a documented nighttime definition within each file and identify LAPD areas, reporting districts, or hundred-block locations with recurring nighttime reported records. Legacy adds 2020–2024 historical context, and NIBRS provides a current offense-level view. The output can be a screening list for further on-site lighting assessment, presented separately by reporting era.
- **What is only partly supported:** The files can prioritize places for inspection, but they cannot determine whether a place is actually dark or a particular light needs repair. Locations are privacy-rounded; both files contain coordinate-quality issues; and the team must define nighttime consistently. The two reporting systems cannot be treated as one uninterrupted series without demonstrating comparability.
- **What is outside the data's scope:** The team cannot use either file alone to prescribe a lighting improvement, select a pole, estimate project cost, judge engineering feasibility, or claim that a lighting change would reduce offenses. These decisions require streetlight inventory, condition, illumination, site, cost, and follow-up outcome data.
- **Association or causation:** The analysis can show that reported records recur at certain locations and times within each reporting system. It cannot conclude that inadequate lighting caused those offenses or that better lighting would prevent them because lighting exposure and intervention outcomes are not observed.

## Comparison and open questions

- **Which candidate fits the observed data better, and why:** Candidate Vision B still fits the two core files better. Both files directly observe time, reporting district, privacy-protected location, coordinates, offense category, and premise type, which can screen locations for on-site assessment. Vision A can use the same descriptive patterns, but allocating outreach resources also requires community needs, existing services, capacity, resident input, and program-effectiveness evidence. Adding Legacy strengthens historical context for both Visions but does not supply those missing decision variables. Vision B still requires separate lighting data before recommending an improvement.
- **What I checked in the source description or rows:** I inspected both downloaded CSVs. NIBRS has 471,285 rows and 31 columns; Legacy has 1,004,894 rows and 28 columns. I checked identifiers, row counts, date ranges, missing values, time formats, coordinates, areas, reporting districts, offense fields, premise fields, and additional Legacy crime-code columns. I also compared yearly occurrence counts, measured the 2024 overlap, and tested whether Legacy `DR_NO` and NIBRS `CaseNo` could serve as a direct join key. Only 518 identifier values match exactly, which is insufficient evidence for a reliable general join.
- **AI-generated hypotheses to investigate:** Recurring nighttime records may be concentrated in street, sidewalk, alley, parking, or transit settings. Some geographic concentrations may reflect population, activity, reporting, or recording differences rather than lighting conditions or unmet outreach need. A documented offense crosswalk, streetlight inventory, service-request history, community-service map, or population source may improve decision relevance. These are hypotheses to test, not findings from the core files.
- **What remains uncertain:** The team must define nighttime, select counting units within each system, decide how to handle questionable times and coordinates, and choose a geographic aggregation level. It must also determine whether any offense categories are comparable across the reporting transition, how to prevent double counting during the 2024 overlap, and whether the 518 matching identifier values truly refer to the same events. Until those questions are resolved, the two files should be cleaned and analyzed separately rather than appended.
